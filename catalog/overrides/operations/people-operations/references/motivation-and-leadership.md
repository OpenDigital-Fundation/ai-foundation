# Motivation, leadership and organisational behaviour

Reference for `operations:people-operations`. Theories to frame people decisions (roles, feedback, incentives, team design). Their evidence varies a lot; use them to ask better questions, and decide with what you learn from the people involved.

| Theory | Author | Idea | Practical use | Evidence and caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hierarchy of needs | Abraham Maslow | Needs from physiological and safety up to esteem and self-actualisation | Check basics (pay, safety, stability) before expecting engagement | Influential, but the strict ordering is not supported as a universal rule |
| Two-factor theory | Frederick Herzberg | Hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, the work itself, growth) create satisfaction | Fixing hygiene removes complaints; motivation comes from the work | Mixed replication; useful distinction nonetheless |
| Theory X and Theory Y | Douglas McGregor | Managers who assume people avoid work build control systems; those who assume people seek responsibility build autonomy | Examine the assumptions behind policies and approval chains | Describes managerial beliefs, not types of people |
| Expectancy theory | Victor Vroom | Motivation = expectancy (effort → performance) × instrumentality (performance → reward) × valence (value of the reward) | Diagnose incentive schemes: is the goal reachable, is the reward linked, does anyone want it? | Well supported as a framework |
| Equity theory | J. Stacy Adams | People compare their input/outcome ratio with others' | Make pay and promotion criteria transparent and consistent | Perceived fairness matters more than absolute level |
| Self-determination theory | Edward Deci, Richard Ryan | Intrinsic motivation needs autonomy, competence and relatedness | Give ownership, feedback that builds skill, and belonging | Strong research base; controlling rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation |
| Situational leadership | Paul Hersey, Ken Blanchard | Adjust direction and support to the person's competence and commitment on the task | Different styles for the same person on different tasks | Popular; limited empirical validation |
| Transformational leadership | James MacGregor Burns, Bernard Bass | Leaders who articulate a vision, inspire, stimulate and develop people | Explain the why of changes; invest in development | Well studied; measured mostly by surveys |
| Psychological safety | Amy Edmondson | A shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking | Leaders admit uncertainty, invite dissent, respond to bad news productively | Strong evidence for learning teams; not the same as comfort or low standards |
| Bounded rationality | Herbert Simon | People decide with limited information, time and attention, and satisfice | Simplify decisions, set defaults, provide decision aids | Foundation of behavioural economics |
| Learning organisation | Peter Senge | Five disciplines: systems thinking, personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning | Retrospectives, shared models of the system, learning reviews after incidents | Conceptual model |

## Applying them

- **Incentives:** check every scheme with expectancy and equity theory, and with Goodhart's law (`operations:project-management`, `references/management-models.md`).
- **Feedback and reviews:** competence (self-determination) needs specific, timely feedback; separate development conversations from pay decisions where possible.
- **Team health:** measure psychological safety with a short validated survey (Edmondson's seven items) before and after changes, and read the comments.
- **Change:** explain the reason (transformational leadership), keep the hygiene factors stable during the change (Herzberg), and give people control over how, if not whether (self-determination).
